# Research foundations for phrase, memory, and tension

BeatFlow uses these sources to guide a compositional workflow, not to claim
that a metric proves musical quality. The engine exposes intent and evidence;
Codex still composes, and listening still decides.

## Meter, accent, and shared time

Meter is a hierarchy of stronger and weaker positions, not merely a list of
quantized coordinates. Experimental and corpus work supports internalized
metrical hierarchies and interaction between tonal importance and metric
position. Dynamic attending theory explains how recurring temporal structure
guides attention, while syncopation research shows that displacement is heard
relative to an already inferred meter.

Practical consequences:

- establish a perceptible tactus and bar frame before foreground displacement;
- align some phrase entries, longer notes, contour peaks, and resolutions with
  structural anchors instead of checking onset legality alone;
- treat a pickup, anticipation, or suspension as departure and consequence,
  not as the permanent phase of every group;
- preserve exact quantization while diagnosing conflicting accent hierarchies;
- use authored metric thresholds as listening hypotheses, not genre rules.

## Perceptual phrase boundaries

Musical boundaries are graded and multi-cue. Temporal gaps are especially
important, but pitch change, register, note lengthening, local parallelism,
metrical position, harmony, and texture can reinforce or weaken a boundary.

Practical consequences:

- declare boundary strength instead of treating every rest as equivalent;
- combine a real gap or handoff with lengthening, thinning, contour closure,
  harmonic arrival, or register change at important endings;
- do not manufacture a fixed rest after every four or eight bars;
- measure the complete selected texture, because alternating rests in
  individual voices can still produce an unbroken stream;
- interpret a diagnostic score as evidence, not as a segmentation oracle.

## Theme identity and memorability

Recognition depends on relationships across time, not a bag of legal notes.
Contour can support memory for an unfamiliar melody, while exact interval and
pitch information become important as familiarity and comparison change.
Research on involuntary musical imagery also suggests that familiar global
contours can coexist with distinctive local turning-point behavior. That
finding concerns popular-song corpora and must not be generalized into a
universal catchiness formula.

Practical consequences:

- design a short subject with a recognizable rhythm and contour before
  generating a long form;
- include an interruption, leap, held tone, repeated-tone figure, or another
  identity-bearing relationship instead of a plain scale;
- restate the subject exactly or nearly exactly before transforming it;
- create multiple structurally different short candidates and select by
  listening;
- preserve identity-bearing relationships during development rather than
  changing one arbitrary pitch.

## Expectation and tension

Expectation can help explain why an event confirms or violates a listener's
learned predictions. Tension is time-dependent and multidimensional: harmony,
pitch height, onset frequency, dynamics, tempo, register, and other features
interact. Neither maximum surprise nor maximum density is a general goal.

Practical consequences:

- declare a phrase-level start, peak, and end tension intention;
- use density only as one observable proxy;
- create local prediction, meaningful deviation, and intelligible
  consequence;
- treat an expected rhythmic slot as part of the idea: omit it only when the
  omission becomes intelligible as syncopation, fragmentation, handoff, or
  closure;
- place the release before the listener has already exhausted the phrase;
- verify by listening whether harmony, contour, register, dynamics, and
  texture jointly express the intended curve.

## Completion and formal function

A grouping boundary and a convincing completion are related but distinct.
Experimental work shows that tonal ending and temporal accent interact in
perceived resolution. Melodic implication studies show that local intervals
create continuation expectations, while phrase-level work finds that both
pitch structure and rhythm influence expectancy. Formal-function theory
distinguishes beginning, middle, ending, and after-the-end behavior rather
than treating all notes inside one group as functionally equivalent.

Practical consequences:

- declare the intended arrival separately from the phrase boundary;
- make the arrival occur at the expected time instead of stopping early and
  padding the remainder with silence;
- budget any echo, link, or afterglow after the arrival;
- compare several plausible stopping points by listening rather than using a
  universal note-count threshold;
- treat local pitch-continuation principles as evidence, not a melody
  generator or culture-independent law.

## Hierarchy and long-form generation

Long sequences benefit from explicit hierarchy. Research systems using
hierarchical latent decoding or relative attention demonstrate why motif,
phrase, section, and form should not be collapsed into one flat event stream.
These architectures motivate BeatFlow's separation of phrase intent, section
material, and timeline arrangement. Meter and event hierarchy are also
distinct: quantization alone does not decide which events deserve attention.
These ideas motivate optional internal phrase stages; they do not guarantee a
good composition.

- Roberts, A., Engel, J., Raffel, C., Hawthorne, C., & Eck, D. (2018).
  A hierarchical latent vector model for learning long-term structure in

  [PMLR](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v80/roberts18a.html)
- Huang, C.-Z. A., et al. (2019). Music Transformer: Generating music with

## Scope

Most cited experiments study Western tonal or popular-music materials.
BeatFlow therefore treats their results as useful evidence for defaults, not
as culture-independent laws. A brief may intentionally request continuous
motor rhythm, elided cadences, drones, additive rhythm, or weak segmentation;
record that intent and interpret the same measurements in context.
